##  Tuesday — Exploring MoMA’s Diversity Claim
On Tuesday, I began examining MoMA’s dataset and researching the museum’s public statements about diversity and global representation. My goal was to test whether its collection truly reflects the inclusivity it promotes.  

I started with the **Artist dataset**, which contains nationality data for each artist. Using this, I created a visualization showing the top 20 nationalities represented in MoMA’s collection. The results were striking — **American artists make up about 45.8% of the museum’s holdings**, nearly half of all artists.  


<img width="1781"  alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/01b46d9d-c2c7-4573-a208-5c98fc528134" />


While MoMA emphasizes global diversity, the data reveals a predominantly **Western-centric collection**, with limited representation from non‑Western regions. This finding became the first chapter of my data storytelling journey, highlighting how numbers can challenge institutional narratives about inclusivity.


### Gender Representation Analysis
This chart compares gender representation across the top 15 nationalities in the museum’s collection. Male artists dominate in every group, though the extent varies. **Brazilian artists** have the highest female representation at **27.6%**, followed by **American**, **Canadian**, **Argentine**, and **Austrian** artists. Meanwhile, **French**, **Italian**, and **Spanish** artists show the strongest male dominance, each exceeding **75%**.  

<img width="975"  alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/1e2af819-5949-4089-a2d4-050b8ac0a82e" />


Although the nationality summary showed that **American artists are the largest group overall (about 45%)**, the gender breakdown reveals a different story: their share of female artists is only **21.8%**, meaning the majority are still male. This highlights that nationality dominance does not automatically translate into gender diversity.

On Wednesday, I continued working with the **Artworks dataset**, focusing on an analysis that linked **classification** with **demographic data**. My goal was to understand which departments had stronger representation of each gender and to explore how MoMA’s collection has grown over the years. At the same time, I researched examples of **storytelling maps** to inspire my own visualization approach. Two resources stood out:  
- MoMA’s *Inventing Abstraction* interactive map, which shows each artwork by artist.  
- Matthew Lincoln’s **Gephi network graph**, mapping places in New York.  

These examples helped me see how networks can reveal relationships beyond simple charts. Inspired by them, I began developing a **network graph** where each artist is grouped by department, and users can click to explore the artworks associated with that artist. This idea combines demographic analysis with interactive storytelling, making the data more engaging and meaningful.

##  Thursday — Refining the Network Map
On Thursday, I continued working on the **networking map**. It took some time to get started, as the main challenge was connecting artists with different departments without the visualization becoming overly cluttered. At first, the graph was crowded with overlapping connections, which made it difficult to interpret.  

To solve this, I experimented with limiting the number of artists visible under each department node. By reducing the density of connections, the map became cleaner and more readable, while still showing the relationships between artists and departments. This adjustment made the visualization more effective and set the stage for further refinement.


<img width="1541" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/62a85ad5-db69-4590-858d-ba299772a557" />


**Reflection:** This exercise reminded me that clarity is just as important as completeness in data visualization. Sometimes showing less information at once makes the story stronger, because the viewer can focus on the relationships that matter most.
